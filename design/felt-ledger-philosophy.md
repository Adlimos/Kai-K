# Felt Ledger

**A visual philosophy of counted chance.**

Felt Ledger treats uncertainty as something that can be surveyed. Every composition begins from a field of deep, matte colour (a green so dark it reads as the quiet of a room after midnight) and builds upward through accumulated, countable marks. Nothing is illustrated; everything is tallied. A form is never drawn once when it can be drawn fifty-two times with patience, and the meaning lives in which few of those marks are allowed to change colour. The work should look meticulously crafted, as if a cartographer of probability had spent weeks inking each unit by hand.

Space is organised around one dominant enclosure: a long oval of concentric hairlines, tight and even as the grain of turned wood. The enclosure is the stage; inside it, small identical units sit in a strict grid, and outside it, scales and ticks extend like the margins of a field notebook. Balance comes from the tension between the soft, continuous curve and the hard, discrete grid it contains. Negative space is generous and deliberate: the darkness around the oval is as much a part of the piece as the marks within it. Every spacing decision is the product of painstaking calibration.

Colour is rationed. The ground is a single deep green. Ivory, the colour of card stock, carries everything known and certain. One metal, a warm brass, is reserved for what is possible but not yet real: the live units, the predicted value, the single moment of hope inside a field of the inert. A cooler, desaturated ivory traces what is merely present. No gradients announce themselves; tone shifts only where material would shift, as felt gives way to rail. The palette should feel like it was mixed by hand and checked under a lamp, with master-level restraint.

Scale and rhythm come from repetition at several sizes. Large forms are drawn as many thin lines rather than one thick one; small forms repeat until they become texture; tick marks run in hundreds so the eye can feel a percentage before reading it. Rhythm is metronomic, broken only once or twice per composition by a mark that is brighter, larger or displaced. Those breaks are the whole point, and they must be placed with the precision of a lifetime's practice.

Text is almost absent. When it appears it behaves like an annotation on a scientific plate: a short figure, a ratio, a reference number, set small and thin, aligned to the same grid as the marks. Occasionally a single numeral may be allowed real size, set in a high-contrast serif, as the one confident statement in a field of careful measurement. Words never explain; they only label what the image has already shown. The result should read as an artifact from an imaginary discipline, the work of someone at the very top of their field who believes chance can be mapped if you are patient enough to count it.
